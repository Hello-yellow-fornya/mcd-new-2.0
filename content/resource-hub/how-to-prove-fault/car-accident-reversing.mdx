## Who is at fault when reversing?

The general starting point is that a driver reversing bears greater responsibility for the manoeuvre. Reversing is considered the higher-risk action: you are moving in a direction you cannot fully see, and the Highway Code (rule 202) requires you not to reverse further than necessary.

That said, this is a starting point, not a fixed rule. If the other driver contributed to the accident, by speeding, by moving into the reversing driver’s path, or by being positioned where they should not have been then fault may be shared or may fall with them entirely.

<Callout lead="The starting point">Reversing drivers carry a greater burden, but it is not absolute. If the other driver contributed, fault can be shared or reversed.</Callout>

## When the reversing driver may not be fully at fault

- The other driver was travelling at excessive speed and could not reasonably have been avoided
- The other driver moved into the reversing driver’s path after the manoeuvre had already begun
- The other driver was parked illegally or in a position that created the hazard
- In a car park, where both drivers share an equal duty of care and neither has automatic priority

## Where the accident happened

### On a road

A driver reversing out of a driveway or side road onto a main road takes on the greater duty of care. The driver already on the main road has priority. If that driver was driving normally and the reversing driver pulled out into their path, fault sits with the reversing driver.

### In a car park

Car parks are shared-use spaces where neither driver has automatic priority on a service lane. Both drivers have an equal duty to take care. Shared fault is common in car park reversing accidents, and the final split depends on what each driver was doing and what they could have seen.

### Into a marked bay

Reversing into a marked parking bay is a controlled manoeuvre. If another vehicle moves into your path while you are partway through a bay reverse, liability depends on who moved last and what each driver could reasonably have anticipated.

### On a motorway or dual carriageway

Reversing on a motorway is illegal under any circumstances. If an accident occurs as a result of reversing on a motorway, the reversing driver is at fault.

## Evidence that helps establish fault

Because reversing accidents are frequently disputed, evidence is important. The position and pattern of damage on each vehicle is particularly useful as it shows the direction of travel and point of impact.

- [Dash cam](/advice/dash-cam-non-fault-accident/) footage, yours or the other driver’s
- CCTV: You will find that car parks almost always have camera coverage
- Witness accounts from people with no interest in the outcome
- Position of damage on both vehicles
- Tyre marks or debris on the road
